# On keeping the margins

## A habit older than the printing press

Montaigne confesses, in [Of Books](../essayists/montaigne/of-books.md), that his memory is so poor he has read books again as new, and that he took to writing a short judgment at the end of each one so that he would at least know what he had once thought of it. The margin, for him, was not decoration. It was a second memory, kept on the object itself.

Woolf's [common reader](../essayists/woolf/the-common-reader.md) does the same thing in a humbler room: reads "for his own pleasure rather than to impart knowledge," and builds, out of odds and ends, "some kind of whole." Thoreau, in [Reading](../walden/thoreau/reading.md), asks for more effort than either of them would admit to, but he too is talking about what a reader carries away.

## What a margin owes the text

A note in a margin makes two promises. It promises to say something true about a passage, and it promises to stay next to that passage. Paper keeps the second promise for free. A digital library does not.

When a source is revised, every note anchored to it has to be placed again. The honest outcomes are three: the passage is still there, it moved, or it is gone. The dishonest fourth outcome, quietly attaching the note to whatever text now occupies the old position, is the one I worry about most, because nobody notices it until a reader trusts a margin that no longer describes what it sits beside.

The Lantern team's [decision to address highlights by their content](../lantern/decisions/adr-007.md) is, in this light, an archival decision as much as an engineering one. A highlight that knows its own quote can say when it has lost its place.

## Keeping what was there

I would add one rule to their design: when a note is orphaned, show the reader the edition it was written against. A margin is a record of a reading, and a reading happened to particular words. The [earlier edition](../lantern/design/sync-design.md) should stay one click away.

## What would change my mind

If re-anchoring turned out to be right almost always, say ninety-nine times in a hundred on real revisions, the cost of showing editions might outweigh the benefit. I have not seen that number measured. Until it is, I would rather show too much provenance than too little.
